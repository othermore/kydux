# Kydux — the story

What the lessons and levels are written from: the school, its people, its spells and the wizard's way through it. For whoever writes a lesson, a level's intro or a hint. Everything here is Kydux's own; nothing recalls a famous wizard, a famous school of magic or the game that inspired Kydux.

## The names

A name that reaches the player is the same in English and Spanish and is never translated: a character, a place, a spell, a password. It is a word of neither language, so that no translation would be wanted, easy to say in both, and short enough for a child to type. A title or a common noun beside a name is translated: *the head, Orla* / *la directora, Orla*.

## The school

**Arvelia** is a school of magic in an old castle on a hill above a lake, with a village at the hill's foot. Children come at the start of autumn, the wizard among them, to learn magic; and magic, in Arvelia, is done by writing instructions, because the castle and everything in it listens to them. A wand lets the wizard cast spells; a broom, once earned, lets them fly; a book from the library teaches a new spell.

Magic in Arvelia is learned by a strict method: try, watch what happens, and try again. A wizard learns by observing the world's reaction to their code. The teachers and friends remind the wizard of this method often.

**The two friends** (D77). The friend who meets the wizard at the gate and
teaches chapters 1 and 3 is of the wizard's other gender: Nuri for a boy
wizard, Teku for a girl. A level, a lesson or a story names that friend
`friend`, and a text writes their name `{friend}` and their gendered words
as `{{boy's words|girl's words}}`. The other friend, of the wizard's own
gender, is the wizard's dormitory mate, and comes into chapters 2 and 3: in
the dormitory and the library, and on the night out of bed. A level or a
story names the mate `mate`, and a text writes their name `{mate}`; their
words follow the wizard's own choice, `{boy's words|girl's words}` (D84).

**The familiars** (D260, D266). In chapter 5 each first-year describes a
familiar as a class, and Orla's word `minka` calls it into the circle; once it
leaves the circle it lives, for good, one a game. A first-year may describe six
kinds, each in its natural colours: a fox, red, white or black; a crow, black or
white; a bat, black or white; a hare, brown or white; a mouse, brown or white; a
frog, green or red. The wizard's familiar has the kind, the colour and the name
the child gives it; a text names it `{familiar}`, and its example in the plans
is Pimu, a red fox. The friend's familiar is always Tolu, a grey hare, a colour
of the hare's kept for the friend, who studies more than anyone; the mate's is
Kesi, of the wizard's own kind in another of its colours, since the mate copies
the wizard's description. A child who names their own Tolu or Kesi finds the
friends' named Rumo and Nabi instead. Orla's own familiar, in the lessons, is
Vesso, an old crow; Dalu is a white crow of a lesson's example. A familiar's
name is said only to those one trusts wholly, since whoever knows it can call it
and give it orders.

Nuri, Teku, Bimo, Eldra and the familiars are actors a level may hold; the teachers and pupils speak
in intros and outros, and Dask patrols as the `teacher` kind.

## The spells

A spell is a word cast with the wand, `wizard.cast("<spell>")` (D42). Each chapter teaches the spells its levels need; a level names the spells it has in `level.json`.

| --- | --- | --- |
| `zelim` | Breaks the seal of a door the wizard stands next to. | 1 |
| `fendra` | Draws the thing named, on a shelf or across a gap, to the wizard's tile. | 2 |
| `orvim` | Opens the naughty child's map, carried, on the plan of the scene: its cells numbered, and every thing and person with its name and its cell (D134). | 3 |
| `kirra` | Lights the room the wizard is in, so that what is in the dark shows. | 3 |
| `osto` | Holds a hole in the floor closed for a few ticks (D167). | 3 |

| `minka` | Cast on an object of a kind of familiar the wizard has described as a class, it calls the familiar's trial, a figure of light, into the tower's circle (D262); cast on the wizard's living familiar, it calls the familiar back to the wizard's side, running by the shortest way, and the familiar hands over whatever it carries. | 5 |

The gate's password, the first word the wizard says in chapter 1, is **`ambrel`**. Another door's password, when a level needs one, is a word of the same kind, written in `story.md` first.

## The wizard's way

**The Opening.** Chapter 1's first story, *The letter*, comes before the game's mechanics and before the wizard is made (D78). It is told at the gate and goes back in the past: some weeks ago, a letter of admission to Arvelia came for the child's first year. They made the journey, and now they stand at the school's gate at dusk, alone, with no idea how to get in. The creator follows it.

**Chapter 1, Arrival.** *Opening beat:* At the closed gate at dusk, the wizard knows no magic. The friend of the other gender, Nuri or Teku, arrives at the same moment, having grown up among wizards, and offers to help. The wizard says that their parents are not wizards, and so they know nothing of magic; the friend answers that a wizard born to parents without magic is extremely rare, a thing to be proud of, and that the friend's own parents are wizards and have taught the friend enough to teach the wizard the basics. The friend lends the wizard a spare wand to be returned later, and teaches them the basics on the spot: what an instruction is, actions, arguments, words, carrying things, and the first spell, `zelim`. The friend also teaches the method: try, watch, and try again. *Closing beat:* The wizard speaks the password `ambrel`, the gate opens, and the castle is reached. The wizard has gained a friend, a lent wand, and the first spell.

**Chapter 2, The library.** *Opening beat:* Ilda receives the new pupils in the great entrance hall and takes them to the dormitory. After spending their first night in their new home and meeting their dormitory mate—taking the free bed beside the mate's—the wizard realizes they must navigate to the parents' association wand bank in the library to borrow a school wand of their own. Wenna keeps the library, and the wizard learns variables, attributes, and finding things by kind. Locked rooms need their keys; `fendra` brings a book down from a high shelf. Eldra asks for help finding her lost books. *Closing beat:* The wizard has earned the right to borrow a wand from the wand bank and gained Eldra's trust; the friend, come to the library for an old book about the castle's passages because of the rumours, knows the wizard's voice through the shelves and comes out to them, meets the dormitory mate, and takes the spare wand back. Looking out the window at dusk, they overhear older pupils whispering what the friend has heard too: at night, the passages of the castle change and come alive.

**Chapter 3, The passages.** *Opening beat:* A knock on the common room's door at night: the friend, looking in the library for books about the castle, has found the naughty child's map folded inside one, a map drawn years ago, the friend thinks, by a pupil who slipped out at night, with a spell word in its corner, `orvim`, which opens it on the plan of the place where the wizard stands. The friend hands it to the wizard, tells the rumours of the passages and of voices in them, and the three, the mate with them, go out to see. With the map the wizard learns to count the castle's cells. In the dark tunnel, where nothing is seen on the scene or on the map, the wizard learns to look at where they stand. At the crossing Dask walks by with his lantern and the wizard learns to wait; by the statues he stands looking up or down, and an expression answered true or false lets the wizard choose (`if`) which way to pass him; in the room of two doors one of the doors is locked, and a program can ask which; three doors alike are one loop (`for`), and Dask's long corridor is crossed by waiting exactly as long as he looks this way (`while`). A hole that opens in the floor is waited for too, while it is open; in the storeroom `kirra` lights the dark and a key is found by its cell. By a crack in the storeroom's right wall they overhear a plot: an older pupil, Rinlo, is being talked round by a cold, strange voice that comes from the stone into tampering with the brooms for tomorrow's flying lesson. On the way down, a hole that never closes on its own, a dark portal, is held shut with `osto`, the one way across (D178). *Closing beat:* On the way down, with a key, a door, that hole and Dask's last corridor behind them, Dask steps out of the dark before the last arch and, impressed by how they looked before every step, lets them go, this once. Back under the blankets, the three share a secret, and a mission to stop the accident the next day.

**Chapter 4, The broom.** *Opening beat:* The morning after, the three have hardly slept: through the storeroom's crack they heard a cold voice talk an older pupil into doing something to the brooms before today's flying lesson, and they cannot tell anyone without owning that they were out of bed. So they go down to the broom shed before breakfast, before anyone else, to put it right themselves. The shed's door is open and someone has been at it: the rack is empty, some brooms lie in a row by the bench and others are scattered in the straw, hardly seen. The friend picks one up and finds, hidden in its twigs, a black knotted thread: an enchanted knot that would make the broom buck in the air and throw its rider. The voice said to do it to every broom. How many are there, and where? The wizard sees them all at once with a list, `find_all`, and counts them with `len`; takes the knot off every broom on the wall with a loop over the list; reaches each one in the straw by comparing its two numbers with the wizard's; and checks them all again with an `if` inside the loop, since untying a knot that is not there is an error. Ilda arrives with the class, and with Zarek, a second-year who already flies and says so. She teaches the word that lifts the broom, `drevo`: flying crosses what walking cannot, and goes much faster. The first flight crosses the lake's arm, from the jetty to the white stone. On the long field three pairs of rings stand with a clump of trees between the first two and a pond between the last two; in broom races the first ring of a pair is flown from below and the second from above, so the same two rings come three times with something different in between, and a loop cannot serve: the wizard gives those lines a name of their own, a function (`def`). *Closing beat:* Ilda lets Zarek and the wizard race on the long course, each in a lane of their own through rings of their own, scored by her count: before the start she reads each program, a tick a line, so a long program takes off late; every ring missed costs five ticks before the finish, and a tie is flown again. The wizard's function takes arguments, since each pair is the same move at another distance, and wins; Zarek asks for a rematch with the rings moved, and the wizard changes only the numbers of the race's program and wins again. Zarek loses well. Ilda, who found the shed in a mess that morning and every broom sound, and has seen no thread, tells the wizard that no first-year has won these races on their first day for many years. Arvelia has a broom racing team; the wizard joins it after winning the race on the first day, and so keeps the broom. At the edge of the field, apart from the class, an older pupil stares at the brooms muttering *...he said no one would get hurt... that it would put an end to the flying lessons...*: it is the one from the crack, and the three do not know his name. The cold voice is still a mystery.

**Chapter 5, The familiar.** *Opening beat:* Months have gone by, and it is the last day of the first year; the wizard flies with Arvelia's broom racing team. The three have kept their secret all this time: the friend keeps the black threads untied in the shed in a little box, often carried along so that nobody finds it by accident, the cold voice has not been heard again, and the older pupil who muttered at the field's edge turns down another corridor whenever he sees them. They do not even know his name until Zarek, who has lost another race to the wizard and a bet with it, to answer any question of the winner's, is asked at the entrance who the fair, tousled boy who never flies is: *That one? Rinlo, from my year. You wouldn't get him on a broom for anything.* The mate wants to tell Orla that very day; the friend answers that a few black threads prove nothing. The first year ends at the top of the tower, with Orla, in the hall where each pupil is given a familiar, a small creature that will go with them through all their years at Arvelia. But no creature waits in the hall: there is a circle of runes on the floor, a great book open on a lectern, and Bimo asleep beside the book. A familiar is not chosen, Orla says; it is conjured, and first of all described. Each pupil says what kind of creature it is, what it knows and what it does, as a class, and the word `minka` calls it into the circle. A first-year may describe six kinds, in their natural colours, with a name, and nothing more; the rest asks for magic they do not have yet, and a description that asks for it stumbles in the circle with Orla's words, never with an error. What comes into the circle is only a trial, a figure of light that shows what was described: it is not alive, it comes undone by itself, and it may be tried again as often as one likes. But a creature taken out of the circle has a soul, and lives, for good; so nobody takes a creature out of the circle until they are sure it is the one they want as their familiar, and Orla looks over every description and every conjuring first. The wizard describes theirs step by step, and at each trial it comes more whole: first a creature of light with no colour and no name, then one with its name and its colour, then one born whole from a single line, since the book of Arvelia conjures each familiar from one line, with everything else in its description: a description written in the book binds the creature, so that it stays the same for its whole life, and one not bound could change into something else or vanish. The friend, who has read everything, makes theirs alone and is the first to take it out of the circle: Tolu, a grey hare, a colour no first-year is given and Orla praises. The mate's trials fail one after another, a frog that will not keep still, a bat they do not like, and a spark with no shape from a kind no first-year may describe, until the mate borrows the wizard's description, copies it on a slate, copies it wrong, and the wizard mends it: from one class come two creatures that differ only in their colour, and the mate whispers their own's name, Kesi, in the wizard's ear, since a familiar's name is told only to those one trusts wholly: whoever knows it can call it and give it orders. While the wizard's trial is in the circle, everything can change; once the program is right, Orla says so and writes the description and its conjuring in the book of Arvelia, where it is conjured for good, and the familiar walks out of the circle and stops being light: it has fur and a shadow, and looks at its wizard, and from then on it is in all the wizard's programs by its name. It is born knowing how to walk, which every familiar inherits; the wizard teaches it to go where it is told, `go_to`, so that Orla, who does not know the wizard's program, can send it to the sun's plate and to the star's. *Middle beat:* As soon as the trial is over, the familiar sniffs at the friend's pocket, where the little box is, darts to a tiny arch at the foot of the wall and comes back with something in its mouth: another black thread, with the same knot. The castle, the friend has read in *Lies and Truths about Arvelia*, by the wizard Juni Tamari, is full of small passages made for familiars to move fast from one side to another without disturbing the castle's other dwellers, so narrow that only a small animal fits in them; the mate is sure that whoever set the brooms' trap is at the other end, and the friend that finding him is the proof they need to talk to Orla. The three go down to the corridor below, where the passage has another entrance, small as a cat flap, and the wizard sends the familiar inside, corner after corner, up to a forgotten room. Rinlo is hiding there. Through the passage, which carries voices like a pipe, they hear the cold voice: it no longer speaks of brooms; it knows who undid the knots and the wizard's name, and asks Rinlo to tie the last thread to the wizard's wand, so that their next spell bursts in their hands. Rinlo says no: nobody was to be hurt, and this could kill. He breaks the thread into pieces and tells the voice to go away; the familiar, born that very day, has run back to its wizard as soon as it heard the voice. The friend calls up to him: they have heard him say no, and will go with him to Orla. Rinlo dares not come out, and still feels the thread there; he wants the pieces taken away, and will not even touch them. The wizard sends the familiar again, and it gathers every piece, wherever it lies, and brings them back. Then a bolt is heard above: Rinlo comes down by the back stair, pale, and tells it all in a rush: flying terrifies him; the voice found him, he thinks, by his fear, and has spoken to him all year from the castle's walls whenever he was alone in a room; it promised him the flying lessons would end and nobody would be hurt, gave him the threads and taught him to knot them, and then kept wanting more, until today he said he would do nothing else. *Closing beat:* The four go up the tower with the threads. The children say they are threads for tampering with brooms, and Orla thanks them for their truthfulness and their courage and tells them they are far more: the leavings of the soul of a wizard who must have been killed by a black spell, evil itself made a thing by that death, which brings death and pain and destroys friendship and the soul; loose they sleep, and the knot wakes them. She locks them away, and believes the children; to Rinlo she says that he has said no and beaten his fears, which was the hardest thing, and to treasure the friendships that make him better. Then she tells the rest: the voice is Korvel's. Long ago he wrote enchantments on the castle itself, to make it better at first, and then, change by change, so that it would obey him alone; Orla and other wizards drove him out. The wizard's parents, wizards and among the best, set about rewriting the castle's spells, keeping the good and taking out what gave him control. Korvel came back to kill them; but the wizard's mother was already expecting the wizard, and the child not yet born protected them both, so that he could kill only the magic in them: that is why they are not wizards now, and remember nothing. Their unfinished spells came undone with their magic, the castle's magic fell, and Korvel died with it, since, seeking to live for ever, he had put his own soul into the castle's spells. It took whole years to mend Arvelia's magic, and Orla always believed him dead; today she is not sure, and says so as a doubt: his soul may be held in the walls, in remnants of old spells, waiting to take revenge and take the castle back. Whose soul the threads are is not said. The mate is the one to see it: then the wizard has beaten him once already. So it is, Orla says: love is a powerful enemy of evil, and there is no greater magic than to love and be loved. She knows now, and Arvelia stays alert; whatever was left of Korvel has most likely gone with the broken thread. At the end-of-year dinner Zarek sits down beside Rinlo, grumbling that Orla has told him to help him with the broom, and Orla names the three pupils of Arvelia in their own right; the familiar sleeps in the wizard's hood. The second year waits.

## The tone

Warm, brave and funny, and scary only a little, once: at the first year's end, when Orla tells what the black threads are, who Korvel was and what he did to the wizard's parents, slowly, in a hall full of sun, with the four together. The castle is old and full of surprises, not of dangers: a teacher who catches the wizard sends them back to try again, a dark room is only dark until it is lit, a ghost is only lonely. The villain is a mystery to be solved, felt rather than seen: a strange voice in the walls, an enchantment that was tampered with, a name the grown-ups treat with caution. He threatens the school and its secrets; once, in chapter 5, he wants harm done to the wizard, and the one he asks refuses him. The children are never hurt and never in real danger on screen; what is at stake is protecting the school and proving what they know. The child is never mocked, and a failure is always the code's, never the child's: *the wizard walked into the wall* rather than *you made a mistake*. Jokes are gentle and come from the characters: Bimo's opinions, Nuri's questions, Teku's hurry, Zarek's boasts.
